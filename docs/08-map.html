The Map

A continuous 3D landscape. No grid, no hexes. You place a building where the ground will take it, and the ground is real terrain with slope, water and a shoreline.

Dimensions

MapSizePlayersTarget gameNote
Duel240 × 240 m225–35 minThree-age skirmish. The one people replay.
Standard420 × 420 m2–460–80 minThe full six-age game
Grand640 × 640 m4–690 min+Later. Needs the flow-field cost to be proven first.

For scale: the existing map_village is 104 × 84 m and holds a dressed Norse village comfortably. A 420 m map is therefore about 20 villages of space — the right amount for four players to expand into without the walk between bases becoming the game.

Terrain construction

The existing lib/mapgen.py and lib/terrain.py already do this well, and the three ideas below are the ones worth carrying forward without modification.

1. Biome as a vertex attribute, not submeshes

One biome BYTE_COLOR attribute on mesh corners: R = biome index 0–1 across [sand, grass, forest, rocky, snow], G = wear. Adjacent biomes interpolate. The alternative — sorting every quad into one of four submeshes — produces stair-stepped boundaries, because a boundary that exists as a discrete edge can step. This one has no boundary to step on.

2. Paths written into the terrain's vertex data, not laid on top

route_network() builds a minimum spanning tree over the structures, adds shortcut edges, applies meander, and bakes the result into the wear channel. No decals, no z-fighting, conforms to the ground for free. Roads built by the player in-game write into the same channel at runtime.

3. review() is an asserting test for placement

Floating objects, sunken objects, overlaps, off-map — all checked with tolerances, and the build fails rather than producing a map somebody has to notice is wrong by eye. This is the flat-map test's discipline applied to geometry, and it is the reason to trust a generated map at all.

Scatter — the mistake to not repeat Uniform tree scatter produces an impenetrable hedge. 864 trees placed evenly formed a wall with no way through and no visual interest. The fix that works: a low-frequency clump mask multiplied into the density, plus a keep-out radius around structures and paths. Forests must have edges, clearings and gaps you can walk through, or the map plays as a maze.

Ground cover is not decoration

Measured, on the last map A dressed village came to 23,085 objects and 3.8 M triangles. Of that, grass tufts were 7,434 objects and 2.92 M triangles — 76% of the entire scene. Flowers, mushrooms and ferns added another 10,400 objects. Against a comparable map with zero ground cover, that difference is most of the gap between “a place” and “a golf course”.

Therefore: ground cover ships as ~42 small meshes plus a cover.json of transforms, GPU-instanced in Unity. Budget 15,000–20,000 instances on a Standard map. This is one DrawMeshInstancedIndirect call per mesh, so the cost is triangles, not draw calls — which is the resource a desktop GPU actually has spare.

Map generation

Maps are generated by script from a seed and an archetype, then reviewed by the asserting harness, then hand-adjusted only through parameters — never by moving things in the GUI, which is lost on the next build.

World scale — validated against a shipped RTS

Verified 3–0 · Supreme Commander 2 SC2 shipped its pathfinding world at 1 grid square = 1 world metre, partitioned into 10 × 10 m sectors joined by portal windows. That is direct, first-hand confirmation from the engineer who shipped it, and it validates our two core conventions: 1 Unity unit = 1 metre, and 1 m flow-field cells.
MapCells at 1 m8-bit cost fieldVerdict
Duel · 240 m57,600~58 KBTrivial

Pathfinding memory is a non-issue at every map size we plan to ship. Note that a more detailed per-cell memory breakdown from the same source was refuted 0–3, so budget from the 8-bit cost field above and measure the rest rather than trusting published per-field figures.

The honest scope note Flow-field tiles decouple planning cost from unit count — A* runs only over a sparse portal graph and tiles are cached across agents and across goals. At a 200-unit cap that full portal/tile architecture is over-engineering. Per-agent steering and local avoidance stay O(agents) and will dominate the frame. Build the plain shared flow field first, profile, and only add portals and tile caching if the profile actually asks for them.

Camera

Desktop RTS camera, and the mistakes from the mobile build are specifically excluded.

InputAction
Right-click drag / middle-click dragPan
Scroll wheelZoom at the cursor, not at screen centre
Trackpad two-finger scrollPan · pinch to zoom (macOS)
WASD / arrow keysPan
Mouse at screen edgePan, toggleable in settings
Q / ERotate yaw in 45° steps
Left-clickSelection only. Never bound to pan.
Refuted 0–3 — do not write this code The widely repeated claim that Windows reports mouse-wheel scroll in steps of ±120 in Unity's Input System (inherited from WM_MOUSEWHEEL) failed verification twice. Do not build cross-platform zoom around a divide-by-120 constant or any other assumed magnitude. The correct normalisation is unknown, and this is now less settled than before the research pass, not more.

What to do instead: a half-day measurement harness that logs raw Mouse.current.scroll deltas for a wheel notch, a macOS trackpad two-finger flick, and a pinch, on both platforms — then normalise from the measurements. This blocks zoom-at-cursor.
On reference implementations No verified best-practice guidance for a desktop RTS camera was found. The one reference repo that surfaced targets Cinemachine 2 on Unity 2022.3 on its default branch, with Cinemachine 3 — the version Unity 6 ships — only on an experimental branch. Its binding set also failed verification (1–2). Treat the camera as unsolved and write it ourselves, with one useful structural idea worth taking: put input behind an interface with interchangeable provider components, so the input backend can be swapped without touching camera logic.

Pitch is fixed at 50°, zoom range 18–90 m above ground, and bounds and zoom limits must respond to aspect ratio — desktop runs 1080p to 4K, windowed and resizable, and a camera tuned for a fixed phone aspect shows the edge of the world at 21:9.
