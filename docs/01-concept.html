01 · Foundation

The Game

Everything else in this folder is downstream of this page. If a later document contradicts it, this one wins until it is changed here on purpose.

One paragraph

North star You start with one citizen and a cooking fire. You finish with a missile silo. Clash of Ages is a real-time strategy game across six ages of technology, played on a continuous 3D map with no grid. One citizen becomes twenty; a longhouse becomes a command centre; a thrown spear becomes a warhead. The whole game is the feeling of a single lifetime of progress, compressed — and the discovery that every age solves the previous age's problem by inventing a worse one.

The core loop

Four verbs, and they never change across the six ages. Only their nouns do.

VerbAge 1 nounAge 6 nounWhat stays constant
GatherCitizen chops a birchDerrick pumps oilA worker stands somewhere and a bar fills
BuildCitizen raises a hutCrane erects a siloSpend resources, wait, a thing appears on the ground
GrowLonghouse trains a citizenCommand centre trains an engineerPopulation is the real currency
ResearchCarve runes at the hallLab simulationKnowledge buys the future, not the present

The loop, stated as a cycle

gather  →  build  →  more workers  →  gather faster
                ↓                            ↓
          military                      KNOWLEDGE
                ↓                            ↓
        take more map  ────────────→  ADVANCE AN AGE
                                             ↓
                              everything above gets replaced

The age advance is the pleasure spike. It is the moment where the player's whole board visibly upgrades. We should spend art and audio budget there before we spend it anywhere else.

Target session: 60–80 minutes for a full six-age game. Also ship a three-age “Skirmish” at 25–30 minutes, because that is the length people actually replay.

The five rules

These are here to settle arguments quickly. Each one is a real choice with a real cost.

1. One citizen at the start. Always.

Not four, not twelve. The opening minute is one person alone in a landscape, and that is the emotional hook of the whole game — the distance between that image and the missile silo is the product. Cost: the first ninety seconds are slow, so they must be beautiful and loud. The first tree felled should be an event.

2. The map is continuous. No grid, no hexes.

Buildings place freely on a heightfield with a footprint and a slope test. Cost: placement, pathing and collision all get harder than a tile game. Benefit: the world reads as a place, and the existing Blender terrain pipeline already produces exactly this. See 08 · Map.

3. Knowledge is generated, never gathered.

No citizen walks to a knowledge pile. Libraries, universities and labs emit it per second, and only while staffed and powered. Advancing an age therefore means deciding, twenty minutes earlier, to build something that produces nothing you can eat or shoot. That decision is the strategic spine of the game. Lifted from Rise of Nations, which got it right.

4. Ages are irreversible and total.

When you advance, the whole civilisation changes — models, materials, unit silhouettes, music, UI trim. You cannot go back and you cannot mix. Cost: 6× the art. Mitigation: ages 1 and 5 are already built (Norse kit and modern kit); see 05 · Assets.

5. Territory is drawn as colour, and you can only build inside your own.

Borders expand from cities and are painted on the ground. Enemy units inside your nation take continuous attrition; yours regenerate. This is the rule that makes it Rise of Nations rather than a base-builder with an age ladder — you fight over the map itself, not over resource nodes. It is also the most expensive rule here, because it adds a map-wide system. Worth it: see 12 · Territory.

6. No timers, no energy, no purchases.

The folder is called clash-of-clans for historical reasons and the name has stuck. The game takes none of the free-to-play scaffolding — no build timers, no energy, no currency. This is a premium PC game that ends.

Armageddon survivalFive nuclear detonations by anyone ends the world. Every nation that has ever launched loses. A non-nuclear survivor wins.This is the answer to Q1 — see 12 · Territory
Resolved — was the project's oldest open question Is using a nuke a win button, or a loss condition disguised as one? Rise of Nations already had the better answer: a shared Armageddon counter. Every detonation by anyone increments it; at five, the world ends and everyone who ever launched loses. The warhead becomes a button that spends a shared, finite, irreversible resource — and the fifth press kills you too. Full rules in 12 · Territory.

What this game is not

  • Not a 4X. No diplomacy screens, no espionage tabs, no trade treaties. You see the map.
  • Not a city builder. Buildings have jobs, not happiness meters.
  • Not multiplayer at launch. Single player versus AI, skirmish and a campaign. Netcode is a whole second project and would eat the art budget. See 10 · Tech.
  • Not mobile. Mouse, keyboard, right-click, scroll wheel, edge pan.
